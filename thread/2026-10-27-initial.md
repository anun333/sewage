Yes, there's a decent amount, though most of it sits in modeling and research. Real day-to-day plant operations software is mostly still proprietary. Here's what I found, grouped by part of the system.

**Sewer lines and collection networks**

EPA SWMM (Storm Water Management Model) is the big one. It's public-domain EPA software that models storm drains and sanitary and combined sewers, and the Open Water Analytics community maintains a version on GitHub, coordinating with EPA's direction. On top of it sits PySWMM, a Python wrapper that lets code read and change system parameters and control infrastructure while a simulation runs. It's been used in Cincinnati and Columbus, Ohio, where it helped cut tens of millions of gallons of combined sewer overflows a year.

The University of Michigan's Open Storm project is in the same family. It's an open-source, end-to-end platform that includes a hardware stack for sensing and control in harsh environments, a cloud platform for coordinating water assets, and a how-to guide on open-storm.org. Their companion library, pystorms, is a Python sandbox of real-world-inspired control scenarios for testing and comparing control strategies. It's basically a benchmark suite for smart sewer and stormwater control.

For mapping and managing sewer assets, the Swiss TEKSI wastewater module (formerly QGEP) tracks pipe attributes like diameter, material, depth, damage, and construction year, and produces plans and statistics. It's built on a PostgreSQL/PostGIS data model and edited through QGIS. One catch: it's designed around Swiss standards, so US use would need adapting. Development also seems to be moving from the old QGEP repo to the newer teksi/wastewater one. The same group has a drinking-water sibling module, QWAT.

**Sewer inspection**

Sewer-ML is an open dataset rather than software, but it's the main open resource here. It has 1.3 million images annotated by professional sewer inspectors from three utilities over nine years, and the code, models, and dataset are all public. The benchmark scores in the original paper left a lot of room for improvement, so automated CCTV defect detection is still an open problem.

**Treatment plants**

QSDsan, from a university research group, is an open-source Python tool for designing and evaluating sanitation and resource recovery systems. It can simulate a full wastewater treatment plant using the standard Benchmark Simulation Model No. 1 (BSM1). It also covers non-sewered sanitation, like latrines and container-based systems, which matters a lot globally.

WaterTAP is a Python-based, open-source library of water treatment models funded through the Department of Energy's National Alliance for Water Innovation. It leans toward desalination and advanced treatment, but it also includes biological wastewater process models.

**Plant controls and SCADA**

Nothing here is wastewater-specific, but general open-source industrial tools like OpenPLC, FUXA, and Rapid SCADA get used for pump stations and small plants. They're also the closest open equivalents to the Alerton- or Johnson Controls-style systems in building automation.

**What's missing**

The biggest gap is the operator side of a plant: lab data entry, process logs, permit compliance reporting, maintenance tracking tied to process data. Utilities mostly buy proprietary packages for that. The modeling side is well covered by open source, but the "run the plant every day" side mostly isn't.
